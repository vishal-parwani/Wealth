Verdict

A real defence electronics business with a real order book, run on a working-capital cycle that swallows every rupee it earns. Revenue went ₹372 cr → ₹562 cr → ₹904 cr and is ₹1,022 cr trailing; net profit ₹31 cr → ₹56 cr → ₹107 cr → ₹115 cr trailing. Operating margin holds at 23–24%. On the P&L this is one of the better growth stories in Indian defence.

The cash flow statement tells a different story. Free cash flow was −₹131 cr, −₹82 cr and −₹357 cr over three years. Operating cash flow was negative ₹130 cr in FY26 against ₹218 cr of operating profit. Inventory sits at 478 days and receivables at 194; the cash conversion cycle is 443 days — the company waits nearly fifteen months between spending on a programme and being paid for it. The ₹536 cr of financing inflow in FY26 is what covered it.

That cycle is partly structural. Missile and naval sub-systems are built to defence-laboratory specifications, delivered against long acceptance schedules, and paid on government terms. Every peer carries a long cycle. Apollo's is at the extreme end even so, and it is not shortening: 605 days, 450, 443.

The order book gives the growth genuine backing — ₹1,704 cr consolidated as at 8 August, including ₹480 cr at Ideal Explosives, against ₹1,022 cr of trailing revenue, and management guides 40–45% revenue growth for FY27 with QRSAM and MIGM programmes potentially adding ₹2,500–3,000 cr. Q1 was revenue ₹251 cr (+88%) but PAT only ₹25 cr (+43%), because margin fell 926 basis points.

At 119x earnings and 10.6x book, with the June quarter's profit lower than March's and the cash cycle unmoved, Hold. High risk stays on.

Business

What it does

Apollo Micro Systems designs, builds and tests electronic and electro-mechanical sub-systems for Indian defence programmes — missile electronics, fuzes, naval warfare systems, avionics and ground support. Its customers are the DRDO laboratories and the defence public-sector undertakings, and its position comes from having been designed into specific missile and naval programmes over many years. Recent acquisitions in explosives — Ideal Explosives, and a stake in Premier Explosives — extend it from electronics into energetics, which is a different and more capital-hungry business.

Moat

Programme incumbency, which in Indian defence is a strong form of it. Sub-systems are qualified against a specific missile or platform by a DRDO laboratory; requalification is a multi-year process nobody undertakes casually, so once Apollo is on a programme it stays there for the programme's production life. The limits are that the customer is a monopsony that sets prices and payment terms, and that programme timing is entirely outside the company's control.

Order book

₹1,704 cr consolidated as at 8 Aug 2026, including ₹480 cr at Ideal Explosives — about 1.7x trailing revenue, and company-disclosed on the Q1 earnings call. The additional ₹2,500–3,000 cr from QRSAM and MIGM is expected by management rather than awarded, and should be treated as a pipeline number, not an order book.

Risks

A 443-day cash conversion cycle at 119x earnings. Three years of profit have produced minus ₹570 cr of free cash flow, funded by ₹793 cr of new money. Every acceleration in growth widens the funding gap, and the equity keeps being diluted to close it.
  • Margin is compressing as revenue accelerates. EBITDA margin fell 926 basis points year on year in Q1; the June quarter's profit was below March's on higher year-on-year revenue.
  • Inventory of 478 days. Improving from 612, but still fifteen months of production sitting on the balance sheet.
  • Single-customer-class dependence. Indian defence procurement decides revenue timing entirely; the ₹2,500–3,000 cr pipeline depends on two programmes being awarded.
  • Almost no active institutional ownership. 1.1% in mutual funds, thirteen of fourteen holders passive, DII holding never above 1.82%. Two analysts cover a ₹14,500 cr company.
  • Promoter below 50% for the first time in the period under review.
  • Acquisition integration. Two explosives businesses bought in quick succession, a different manufacturing discipline from electronics, with their own working-capital needs.

What would change the rating

  • Upgrade to Buy: one full year of positive free cash flow, or the cash conversion cycle below 350 days with revenue still growing 40% — proof that scale is finally shortening the cycle rather than lengthening the funding need. The QRSAM or MIGM award landing as a confirmed order would strengthen it further.
  • Downgrade to Sell: another equity raise on top of FY26's, EBITDA margin below 18%, or the order book failing to grow past ₹1,704 cr as the current book converts.
