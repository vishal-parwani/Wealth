Verdict

The growth is spectacular and none of it has turned into cash yet. FY26 revenue was ₹301 cr against ₹93 cr two years earlier, net profit ₹46 cr against ₹14 cr, return on capital 28%. On the reported numbers this is one of the better small companies on the exchange. Then look at the cash flow statement: operating cash flow was minus ₹40 cr in FY26 and minus ₹19 cr in FY25. Free cash flow was minus ₹87 cr. The company has reported ₹72 cr of profit across two years and consumed ₹59 cr of operating cash in the same period.

Where it went is visible in the balance sheet. Trade receivables went from ₹17 cr to ₹135 cr in two years and inventory from ₹36 cr to ₹103 cr. Debtor days are 164, inventory days 176, and the working-capital cycle has stretched from 146 days to 256. Growth of this shape is normal for an EMS business scaling into new customers — it is also exactly what a receivables problem looks like from the outside, and there is no way to tell the two apart from published data alone.

The operating story is real. The order book is ₹604 cr, roughly double last year's revenue; FY26 order bookings were up 176%; Q1 FY27 revenue rose 94% to ₹84 cr. A US acquisition and the new Vadodara plant are credited with about ₹500 cr of incremental revenue potential. Promoters hold 70.90% with zero pledge and have been buying.

At 75x earnings and 14.7x book, with cash flow negative and a stock that has gone from ₹660 to ₹1,675 in a year, this needs the cash to arrive before the price makes sense. Hold, high risk.

Business

What it does

Incorporated in 2011 and based in Vadodara, Aimtron designs and manufactures printed circuit boards and assemblies. The strategic move under way is up the value chain — from board assembly for a customer's design, into full electronics manufacturing services and electronics system design, where the company owns more of the engineering and can charge for it. End markets are industrial electronics, power and energy, ruggedised electronics, IoT and robotics, battery energy storage and medical devices. A US subsidiary gives it a Western customer-facing presence.

Peers

The listed Indian EMS set — Kaynes, Syrma, Dixon, Avalon — is five to fifty times Aimtron's size and trades on the same kind of multiple, so a peer table comparing them would flatter Aimtron's growth rate while hiding that it is a fraction of their scale and has none of their institutional following. It is omitted rather than presented misleadingly. The relevant comparison is Aimtron's own cash conversion against its own reported profit, which is in the financials above.

Moat

None yet, and it is important to say so plainly. Contract electronics manufacturing is a scale-and-execution business: customers dual-source, contracts reprice, and the barrier to a competitor bidding is low. What Aimtron is trying to build — design capability that makes it the originator rather than the fabricator of a board — would be a moat if it works, because a design-owned product is far stickier. That transition is a plan supported by an order book, not an established position.

Order book

₹604 cr consolidated order book as at Q1 FY27 — about 2.0x FY26 revenue. FY26 order bookings of ₹521.2 cr were up 176%. This is company-disclosed in the quarterly business update rather than audited, and no conversion schedule is published, so treat it as visibility rather than as contracted revenue with dates attached.

Risks

Two years of profits, none of them in cash. ₹72 cr reported, ₹59 cr of operating cash consumed, receivables up ₹118 cr. At 75x earnings, if the receivables prove slow or uncollectible the reported earnings base disappears and there is no valuation support beneath it.
  • No institution owns it and no analyst covers it. Zero mutual fund holding, 0.22% FII, no published broker estimate. Nothing external is validating these accounts.
  • Half-yearly reporting. You find out how the business is doing twice a year, so a deterioration can run for six months before it is visible.
  • Working capital cycle of 256 days and lengthening. Growth at this rate with this cycle needs continual funding — ₹88 cr of financing came in during FY26 alone.
  • Liquidity. 0.5 lakh shares and ₹5.9 cr a day on a ₹3,500 cr company. Any exit of size is a multi-day operation at prices you do not choose.
  • Single-person control. ~65% with one individual, 71% with the promoter group.
  • Margin already stepped down from 26% to 20% as the international and manufacturing subsidiaries were built, and the company has flagged that as the reason.

What would change the rating

  • Upgrade to Buy: one full year of positive operating cash flow with debtor days under 120 — that single change would convert this from a reported-profit story into a real one. A first mutual fund taking a disclosed position would be corroborating evidence.
  • Downgrade to Sell: debtor days past 200 again, a receivables write-off, operating cash flow negative for a third straight year, or the order book failing to convert into H1 FY27 revenue.
