Verdict

Buy — unchanged, and the central objection has been resolved. The prior note rated this Buy at ₹136.40 on 13 Aug 2026 with a clear caveat: "a working-capital model that quietly eats the profit it reports". The stock is ₹143.41, up 5.1%. The FY26 cash flow statement says the caveat no longer applies.

FY26 operating cash flow was positive ₹142 cr, against reported PAT of ₹60 cr. That is cash conversion above 200%, after four years of negative or negligible operating cash (−₹269 cr, −₹14 cr, +₹3 cr, −₹21 cr). At the same time the cash conversion cycle fell from 118 days to 71, debtor days from 155 to 140, and days payable rose from 39 to 71 — the company is now collecting sooner and paying later. The last note argued growth pre-funds developers' balance sheets. In FY26 it stopped doing so.

Trading performance is intact. FY26 revenue ₹1,067 cr, up 39%, with PAT of ₹60 cr against ₹6 cr. Q1 FY27 revenue ₹291 cr, up 37%, PAT ₹20 cr against ₹5 cr, operating margin 11% against 9%. The turn from three straight years of losses (FY22–FY24) to ₹75 cr of trailing profit is genuine.

What keeps the high-risk tag. Institutions have gone — FII from 9.79% to 1.94% over the year, DII from 5.77% to 1.09% in a single quarter, and no mutual fund holds a share. Some of that is mechanical post-IPO lock-up expiry, but not all of it, and it leaves no institutional support under a stock that still trades on a thin, largely retail register.

Business

Nature. ArisInfra is a B2B technology-enabled distributor of construction materials — aggregates, cement, steel and related inputs — sold to developers and contractors. It is a margin-thin, volume-heavy intermediation business: buy materials, finance the customer, deliver, collect.

Where the money is made. Operating margin is around 11%, which is respectable for distribution and is the number that improved most over the last two years, from 4.5% in the March 2025 quarter. Scale gives purchasing power; technology is meant to give route density and working-capital control. FY26's cash conversion suggests the second part is starting to work.

The structural risk in the model. This business finances its customers. It extends materially more credit than it receives, so growth consumes cash unless payable terms improve in step — which is exactly what happened in FY26, with days payable going from 39 to 71. Whether the company can hold those supplier terms while continuing to grow 35%+ is the single question that determines whether the last two years were a turnaround or a good patch.

End-market dependence. Revenue tracks Indian real-estate and infrastructure construction. A developer credit squeeze would hit volumes and receivables at the same moment.

Risks

  • Customer credit. 140 debtor days on ~11% margins. Improving, but still means every rupee of growth carries roughly five months of financing.
  • Supplier terms may not hold. Days payable nearly doubled to 71 in FY26. Much of the cash-flow improvement comes from that. If suppliers pull terms back, the cycle re-widens.
  • No institutional ownership. Zero mutual funds, DII at 1.09%, FII at 1.94%. Nobody professional is underwriting this price.
  • Short profitable history. Three consecutive loss years to FY24. Four profitable quarters is not yet a record.
  • Real-estate cycle. A downturn in developer activity hits volume and collections together — the worst combination for this model.
  • Thin margins. At ~11% operating margin, small moves in input cost or price competition swing profit sharply.
  • Data conflict. The debtor-days series here disagrees with the previous note's figures. The direction is favourable on this basis, but the levels should be checked against the annual report before relying on them.

What would change the rating

  • Stays a Buy — 17.8x for 37% revenue growth, positive operating cash flow, a halved cash cycle and a net-cash balance sheet.
  • Raise conviction — a second year of positive operating cash flow, or the first mutual fund taking a position. Either would confirm FY26 was structural.
  • Downgrade to Hold — operating cash flow turning negative again, debtor days rising back above 160, or days payable falling under 50.
  • Downgrade to Sell — a return to losses, or a developer credit event hitting receivables.
